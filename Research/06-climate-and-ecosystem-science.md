# Climate & Ecosystem Science — Grounding the "Problem"

This document backs the game's own framing: why CO2 is a problem worth building a whole
farm/biofuel game about. It replaces the original capstone slide's stat ("people are releasing
4% to 5.3% of CO2 per day") with real, sourced numbers — that figure isn't a coherent
measurement (no defined baseline or unit). The closest real statistics are below.

Every fact here is cited to a primary source (NOAA, NASA, IEA, EPA/IPCC, USGS, or a
peer-reviewed paper). Where a **Narrative phrasing** line follows a fact, that's a suggested way
to phrase it for in-game text — keep the two separate; the citation is what's true, the
phrasing is a suggestion.

---

## 1. Atmospheric CO2: where we are and how fast it's rising

- **Current level: ~429 ppm** (July 2026 monthly mean at Mauna Loa Observatory, Hawaii). The
  2024 global annual average was 422.8 ppm — a new record.
- **Pre-industrial baseline: ~280 ppm**, steady for roughly 6,000 years before the Industrial
  Revolution (from ice-core records).
- **We're now ~50% above that baseline.** This is the real number the capstone slide was
  probably reaching for.
- **Rate of increase: ~2.6 ppm/year** over 2015–2024 (up from 0.8 ppm/year in the 1960s). 2024
  alone saw a record single-year jump of 3.75 ppm.
- **Historical context:** ice cores show CO2 never exceeded 300 ppm in the last 800,000 years —
  until now. The current rate of increase is **100–200× faster** than the rate at the end of the
  last ice age.

**Narrative phrasing:** *"Right now, there are about 430 molecules of CO2 in every million
molecules of air — more than at any time in human history. Before factories and cars, that
number sat steady near 280 for thousands of years. We've pushed it up by half, and we're adding
more every year faster than nature ever has in 800,000 years."*

*Sources: NOAA Global Monitoring Laboratory (gml.noaa.gov/ccgg/trends); NOAA Climate.gov,
"Climate change: atmospheric carbon dioxide"; Lüthi et al. 2008, Nature (ice-core record).*

## 2. How much CO2, from where, and what biofuels can honestly do about it

**Global emissions (2024, several reconciled totals depending on what's counted):**

| Measure | Amount |
|---|---|
| Energy-related CO2 | ~38 billion tonnes/year |
| Fossil CO2 only | ~37.4 billion tonnes/year |
| Fossil + land-use change | ~41.6 billion tonnes/year |
| All greenhouse gases (CO2-equivalent) | ~53 billion tonnes/year |

**By sector (global GHG, IPCC AR6 breakdown):**

| Sector | Share |
|---|---|
| Electricity & heat | 34% |
| Industry | 24% |
| Agriculture, forestry & land use | 22% |
| Transportation | 15% |
| Buildings (direct) | 6% |

**What biofuels can realistically offset — the honest version:** biofuels supply about
**3.5–4% of global transport energy today**. In the IEA's own aggressive Net Zero scenario,
that could reach ~9% by 2030. This game's own subject matter is real and growing, but it is a
**partial** solution, not a total one — it works alongside electrification, efficiency, and
other clean-fuel pathways. Don't let in-game narrative imply biofuel alone "solves" transport
emissions; the honest framing is "a real, meaningful piece of a much bigger kit."

**Narrative phrasing:** *"Every year, human activity releases about 40 billion tonnes of CO2 —
roughly a quarter from making electricity, another quarter from industry, and about 15% from
moving people and goods around. Biofuels like the ones you'll grow here already replace a few
percent of transport fuel, and could reach nearly 10% by 2030 — a real help, but not a cure-all."*

*Sources: IEA, Global Energy Review 2026; Global Carbon Budget 2024 (Friedlingstein et al.,
Earth System Science Data 2025); U.S. EPA, "Global Greenhouse Gas Overview"; IEA, "Biofuels"
and "Delivering Sustainable Fuels."*

## 3. The greenhouse effect, correctly simplified

CO2 absorbs heat radiating up from Earth's surface and re-releases it in all directions,
including back down — a natural effect that keeps the planet above freezing. Humans are
**amplifying** that effect by adding more CO2 than natural sinks can absorb. NOAA estimates CO2
alone accounts for ~80% of the warming influence of all human-produced greenhouse gases since
1990.

**Narrative phrasing:** *"Sunlight warms the Earth, and the Earth sends heat back up toward
space. CO2 in the air acts like a blanket — it soaks up some of that rising heat and radiates
a chunk of it back down. A little CO2 is a good thing: without it, our planet would be frozen.
The problem is we've been adding a lot more of it, so the blanket is getting thicker — and
Earth is running a fever."*

*Sources: NOAA Climate.gov; NASA, "Climate Change: How Do We Know?"*

## 4. Ocean acidification & marine ecosystems — the "marine type stuff"

This is where the game's own "marine ecosystems disturbed" framing gets concrete:

- The ocean absorbs **~30% of the CO2** humans release. Dissolved in seawater, it forms a weak
  acid, lowering pH and making carbonate ions — the raw material shellfish, corals, and plankton
  use to build shells and skeletons — less available.
- **Surface ocean pH has dropped from 8.21 to 8.10 since the Industrial Revolution — a 30%
  increase in acidity**, happening at least 10× faster than any time in the last 50 million
  years.
- **Concrete example — Pacific Northwest oyster crisis:** starting in the late 2000s, CO2-rich
  water upwelling onto the Washington coast began dissolving oyster larvae's shells before they
  could form, killing billions of them and hitting a $270M/year industry directly. This ties
  ocean chemistry straight to a real economic and biological consequence — a strong, concrete
  anchor for game narrative (and it's directly chemistry-themed, which fits this game).
- **Coral bleaching:** the 4th global mass coral bleaching event (confirmed by NOAA, April 2024)
  hit **~84% of the world's coral reef area** across 83+ countries between 2023–2025 — the worst
  event on record. Mechanism: when water gets too hot, corals expel the symbiotic algae that
  give them color and food; if heat persists, they starve and turn white.

**Narrative phrasing:** *"About a quarter of all the CO2 we emit gets swallowed by the ocean.
There it turns into a weak acid that's already made seawater 30% more acidic than before
factories existed. That acid eats the shells of baby oysters and dissolves the skeletons of
corals. From 2023 to 2025, heat and acidity bleached 84% of the world's coral reefs — the worst
event scientists have ever recorded."*

*Sources: NOAA Ocean Service, "What is Ocean Acidification?"; NOAA PMEL, "Ocean Acidification's
impact on oysters and other shellfish"; NOAA Coral Reef Watch, "Current Global Bleaching: Status
Update"; International Coral Reef Initiative.*

## 5. Wildlife on the move — broader biodiversity impact

Distinct from ocean acidification: climate change is causing a **global redistribution of
life**, as species shift poleward, upslope, or deeper to stay within tolerable temperatures.

- Marine species track warming water **~6× faster** than land species — an average of ~70 km
  per decade. An estimated 25–85% of marine species have already shifted range.
- **Concrete U.S. example:** off the northeastern U.S., lobster, red hake, and black sea bass
  have moved north an average of 137 miles since the early 1970s.
- **Flagship example — polar bears:** the Southern Beaufort Sea polar bear population declined
  ~40% in under a decade in the 2000s, driven by sea-ice loss (bears need ice to hunt seals).
  Arctic summer sea-ice extent has declined ~14%/decade since 1979. Polar bears were listed as
  threatened under the U.S. Endangered Species Act in 2008 specifically because of this.
- **Land example:** the American pika, a cold-mountain mammal, is being pushed upslope out of
  lower-elevation habitat in the Sierra Nevada and Great Basin as temperatures rise — a clean
  example of "nowhere left to go" once you reach a mountaintop.

**Narrative phrasing:** *"As the planet warms, wildlife is literally on the move. Fish off the
U.S. East Coast have shifted north by over a hundred miles; polar bears in the Arctic have lost
a quarter of their sea-ice home in a single decade, and one population fell by 40%. Mountain
animals like the American pika are being pushed higher and higher up the slopes — and when you
reach the top, there's nowhere left to go."*

*Sources: Pecl et al., "Biodiversity redistribution under climate change," Science 2017; U.S.
Global Change Research Program, "Marine Species Distribution"; USGS, Southern Beaufort Sea
polar bear population studies; PLOS One, American pika habitat studies.*
